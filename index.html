THE CAPSULE

In 2025, we placed a capsule in hopes that a future KU campus has emerged out of the environmental crisis we now face…

Near the Chemical & Petroleum Engineering building on campus a capsule will be placed. The capsule is created out of resin-based pitch and ceramic, which will be a environmentally-friendly design. Seeing how (in)effective that design is, and the detoriation of the materials inside over that time period, will be a learning experience for the students uncovering the capsule.

The following items will be contained in the capsule;

1. Paper Money

Current function: This is used as a offline medium of exchange. It represents currency. It is also currently the best mix of privacy and vendor acceptance, as dollars are not unique.

Three E’s:

  1. Social Equity: Paper money is very equitable. It is something that can be used and stored without an expensive digital device. It can be exchanged without an internet connection.
  2. Economy: Printing money costs… well, money! Every one or two dollar note takes about 4 cents to make.
  3. Environment: Paper money takes a lot of paper, pulp, and thusly trees to make. In fact, at least one study provided the result that a dollar coin is actually better for the environment than the dollar bill!

Prediction after 100 years: I don’t think it will be used. Maybe paper money will be replaced by coins. Or perhaps, and I think this to be quite likely, it will be replaced almost entirely by digital currency. For ‘physical’ or offline transactions I predict they will utilize smart cards that hold hashes or proofs of holding a specific amount of currency.

2. Printed KU Policy Article

Description: This is a KU policy article printed, specifically this page mentioning asbestos.

Current function: This policy document outlines the responsibilities of KU in regards to health, environment, and safety. Of particular note is the fact that they mention asbestos, a material that has been restricted for years, and fully banned as of last year. In a hundred years, will asbestos even be something worth mentioning as a responsibility of KU in regards to health and safety?

This article impacts the daily lives of students because it dictates how health and safety in regards to the enrivonment, pollutants, and other contaminants, are handeled.

Three E’s:

  1. Social Equity: Quite equitable in function (delegating tasks whose purpose is to ensure the health and safety of people on campus), but not equitable in comprehension. The document is half legalese, half academic writing. It’s not particularly adept at explaining all the roles and functions to the layperson.
  2. Economy: While it is hard to assess the exact economic impact, it is certainly expensive by nature to have so many specialized task forces. Is this truly necessary, or is there a more efficient unified way of tracking all these important subjects?
  3. Environment: Incredibly important to the function of the environment on campus. However; the environment checks seem more concerned with immediate person safety than overall ecosystem health.

Prediction after 100 years: I do not believe this document will exist in its current form a hundred years from now. To begin with, I don’t think several of these concerns, particularly lead paint and asbestos, will even be on the radar of a newer iteration of the document in a hundred years. Further, I’d like to believe that we’d have a much more straightforward method of checking, holistically, the environmental and safety conditions of an area. This solution would be under one major task bureaucratic structure.

3. Weather Radio

Description: This is a specialized radio tuned to specifically radio bands that are related to weather announcements. Usually from the government or some other kind of public service. They are often individually specialized for specific regions of the world and their local circumstances. The design is to allow people to have a convenient and consistent line of communication about natural disasters and other hazards of that variety.

Many people I know personally use weather radios, including my own family and at least two others.

Three E’s:

  1. Social Equity: Very equitable. They cost about 10-30$ and last a very long time. Many of them are hand-crank powered and don’t rely on an external power source whatsoever.
  2. Economy: On the other hand, having purpose-built electronic radios simply for the function of catching a very specific FM radio bandwidth is inefficient. The amount of production and resources required to continually produce such things in the midst of people owning many other highly advanced devices could be describd as absurd.
  3. Environment: Electronics take a lot of resources to mak. Conductive materials such as copper, gold, are rare earther minerals in constant demand. They take a lot of water to mine and result in poisoned water and local environmental destruction. Additionally, E-waste of discarded devics is a huge problem.

Prediction after 100 years: I don’t think weather radios will exist. This will almost certainly be merged in with the existing, highly portable and powerful handheld device we have on us at all times: the phone. This is already partially done; weather warnings are automatically propagated and displayed on devices constantly.

4. Portable Solar Panel

Current function: Solar panels produce electricity from exposure to solar radiation. Particularly, strong wave lengths such as those in the UV band. Small, portable solar panels are used for electricity and recharging on the go. Usually for less intensive tasks such as charging your phone or some other low power device.

College students are able to use portable solar panels to charge their phones and other devices when not near outlets (there are unfortunately many such places on campus, and of course many more off of it).

Three E’s:

  1. Social Equity: Its value from the peerspective of Social Equity is arguable. On one hand, solar panels are incredibly cheap to install and create compared to other methods of generating energy. It also doesn’t require lots of manual exertion like with hand cranks or other physical ways of generating current. On the other hand, portable electrical generation is not very cost efficient for most people. Furthrmore, installing larger scale solar panels in your house is an incredibly expensive, multi-year investment. They tend to pay off rather quickly depending on the location, though.
  2. Economy: Solar panels are great for the economy! A lot of solar installations are much cheaper than the existing maintenance costs of current coal plants. They are certainly a boon in this area.
  3. Environment: These are great improvements over coal and oil for electricity sources.

Prediction after 100 years: I think that not will solar panels be here after one hundred years; they’ll be massively iterated on. Even as it stands, solar energy adoption is rapidly, and without fail, increasing. We also still haven’t reached the absolute constraints of how much energy the panels could collect. Clams, for instance, have incredibly advanced methods of catching the most amount of energy out of sunlight.

5. Tailpipe

Current function: The exhaust pipe is a component of your car that carries the remaining pollutants (largely carbon dioxide) out of the car. This only exists on, and because of, ICE (Interal Combustion Engine) cars, which largely use fuel sources like gasoline. If we didn’t have gas-powered cars, we wouldn’t have tailpipes.

Three E’s:

  1. Social Equity: The tailpipe itself doesn’t have a social equity relation, but its reason for existence does. Pollutants emitted into the air out of the tail end of a tailpipe disproportionately effect people in poorer neighborhoods. Who, on top of having other sources of pollution and poor air, cannot afford the proper medical care and treatment they need to address these issues.
  2. Economy: The price of a tailpipe (and especially of a catylic converters, which have Rhodium an expensive rare earth mineral inside) is an unnecessary. It is a burden on manufacturing. As is the other various parts of the vehicle to do with ICE.
  3. Environment: The carbon dioxide contributed to the atmosphere directly out of an exhaust pipe is significant and a major driver of climate change. Other pollutants also stick around and lead to poor quality air for the people living in the city

Prediction after 100 years: I don’t think we will have tailpipes because I don’t think we will have ICE vehicles. To begin with, they are already being encouraged to be phased out my many major countries, especially in the EU. Secondly, cars are inefficient, and especially ICE cars. The space-passenger ratio is incredibly poor for the vehicles we put up. I dont think th future will be as inclined.

6. Seed packet

Current function: Seeds contained in this packet can be used to grow many thing, including food. Even in your very own backyard! More interestingly, though, is the particular strains of plant we contain within them. These have have been cultivated and specially bred for centuries to better support human life. How will the plants of the future look?

Three E’s:

  1. Social Equity: Seed packets are amazing for social equity. Home gardens provide produce at microscropic fractions of the price you would get them for at the grocer. They have incredible democratizing power, especially if done by major portions of the neighborhood communally. They democratize food production!
  2. Economy: Home gardens and the seed(s) (packets) that produce them are very economically efficient. Much, much more than lawns, which by default take a lot to sustain (they are an artificial monoculture) and do not produce something direct for you as a result.
  3. Environment: Home gardens on top of being much more economically efficient are also much more environmentally efficient than normal lawns, which are monocultures. Seed packets themselves are also much better as a way to move plants places. Fully grown plants are much heavier, require more material packed in, and a much larger container. Thusly they are much more environmentally costly to ship places. However, they are often stored in plastic, single-use packets.

Prediction after 100 years: Seed packets will almost certainly be a thing. They might change form (the single-use packets are not particularly environmentally-friendly). The basic design is very efficient, though, and unlikely to be significantly improved upon beyond the creation of new strains of plants which will have differently designed seeds.

7. A photo of an oil spill

Current function: Oil is used for a great many things in our current lives. It is turned into plastic, which is both what our clothes are often comprised of, and also what a great many of our products are contained within. We also use oil as fuel, such as refining it into gasoline for our cars. Unfortunately, it often spills, and results in intense environmental destruction wherever it does.

And, perhaps (to some) more pressingly, it is of limited supply. We are running out of oil and at our current pace, it will be soon.

Three E’s:

  1. Social Equity: Communities with less resources and poorer household income are disproportionately effected by oil spills. They have less money to coordinate clean-ups and, certainly, less money to offset the harms oil spills cause to their livelihood. For instance, in some places clean water is manually carried from a fresh water source back to their home. If an oil spill happens there… well, there’s not much they can personally do. They have meager resources.
  2. Economy: Oil is inefficient economically for many reasons. For one, it is in a very limited supply. Gone, for the most part, are the types of oil sources that would sprout into the sky upon being accessed. The latest in oil extraction today is ‘fracking’, which involves drilling into th ground and sucking out miniscule amounts of oil from many places to get a sizable total. It’s terrible.
  3. Environment: Oil spills and oil extraction operations massacre local wildlife. It is so bad that Dawn dish soap advertises being used as something to remove oil from the local wildlife it clings to. Beyond the individual, massive oil spill disasters, oil when burnt produces awful pollutants. Including the dreaded CO2 driving the global warming disaster. Prediction after 100 years: Oil will be out of the picture for the vast majority of things. To begin with, our current status on oil is quite declining. Oil is a limited resource that takes hundreds of years to accumulate into something notable. To drain and burn and process it all would lead us off a cliff developmentally. I am very certain it will be phased out.

9. A photo of the Great Pacific Garbage Patch

Background: We live lives full of plastic at every step. Even, and maybe especially, medical care is filled with plastic everywhere. It is so ubiqitious we find it in our blood. Plastic is both what our clothes are often comprised of, it is so much of our machinry is fashioned with, what so much of our appliances use for structural integrity, and also what a great many of our cosummable products (such as food) are contained within.

Oil is turned into plastic, and as such this contributes to its scarcity, as well as inherits its environmental issues.

Three E’s:

  1. Social Equity: Plastic is cheap (but perhaps artificially so, we subsidize the oil industry a lot). Also, plastic recycling and collection of plastic trash is disproportionately present in richer neighborhoods and areas. In many countries outside the West, plastic can be seen collecting in mass in natural areas. This isn’t merely a difference in culture; in some areas this involves being the unfortunate downstream of where a lot of plastic waste ends up.
  2. Economy: Plastic currently serves a very important role economically; it is a cheap, versatile material that can be quickly produced and shaped into various forms and durabillities and qualities. It will be very hard to full replace as-is.
  3. Environment: Plastic often cannot break down naturally in the environment over a decent timeframe. Many types of plastic will last hundreds of years before finally breaking down. Many more will still persist in microscopic forms in the environment for ages. These microplastics effect, at times, biological and environmental processes. Furthermore, the production of plastic currently involves a massive amount of oil; a limited, non-environmentally friendly resource.

Prediction after 100 years: I don’t think plastic as we know it will exit in a hundred years. Many plastic-like uses will be replaced and the plastic-like materials that will take current plastic’s place will be biodegradable. This, I believe, will come as a result of environmental and clutter-related pressures: plastic isn’t good for the environment. And it’s also a pain in the ass to physically manage when it has left its consumer lifecycle.

9. A World War II propaganda poster

Item: Image of old school World War II propaganda for a suburban home

Background: Suburban homes provide larger dwelling outside main urban areas. They usually have larger lawns and are traditionally considered to be ‘better experiences’ for single families. Historically they were quite popular, and to some degree today their affordability is used as a benchmark of the experience of the working class.

A lot of how lives played out, and were expected to play out, made it so that people could expect to buy a suburban house and move their family there, etc. Even (and maybe especially) college graduates after working a job for a little. Not anymore.

Three E’s:

  1. Social Equity: Suburban homes are currently unaffordable to most working class people today. It is an absolutely incomparable situation from 5 decades ago. They have significant property taxes, their lawns (if upkept in the traditional manner) are expensive to maintain, and are generally not within a walkable distance from many city amenities (such as the bus system).
  2. Economy: Suburban homes are inefficient to produce. Their occupancy-land ratio is terrible and their infrastructure is sprawling and disproportionately expensive. Their lawns, if maintained traditionally, are also a terrible drain on resources such as water.
  3. Environment: As iterated on above they waste a lot of water. Furthermore the constant insane amount of land use neccesitate a lot of development and thus destruction of zones where there is nature. Housing should, for the purposes of being environmentally friendly, be compact and have a relativly light influence on the shape and nature of the land surrounding it.

Prediction after 100 years: I don’t think suburban homes will be nearly as common as they are now. I think a lot of what is currently suburbia will be reclaimed as fully urban area, and will be replaced by multi-family homes and apartments. The reason being that the current housing market will not want to be repeated. Furthermore, the current suburban distribution is a particular consequence of during and post world war design meant to encourage the war effort and provide economic relief. We wanted everyone to have a little kingdom, effectively, and heavily pandered to the illusions of grandeur that British displays of wealth generated.

10. Picture of the KC Streetcar

Current function: Streetcars, are interestingly enough, a rather complicated topic. In some senses streetcars mostly proliferated in response to limited oil and rubber rations in World War II, since buses have more flexible lines and capabilities. At the same time, streetcars have an advantage of being largely much more consistent in their schedules, much smoother travel, and usually much larger passenger capacity. They are mostly not in use, today. With a few exceptions, notably Kansas City!

Three E’s:

  1. Social Equity: Most streetcars are free or incredibly cheap to use for the people in their cities.. They serve people rather equally as a result of this economic affordance. They have predictable time schedules which make them convenient and easy to form habits around.
  2. Economy: Street cars have significant upfront investment compared to buses and bus lines, which make them harder to advertis to cities and to maintain. Unlike buses, they require physical modification to infrastructure just to changes lines or t add them. Buses may need dedicated stop infrastructure for optimal rider experience, but are otherwise very light on the upfront investment
  3. Environment: Street cars, unlike combustion and electric buses, are not powered by combustion AND do not require a battery. They are powered directly by eletric lines connected to them, and thusly the grid. In this sense they are actually much more environmentally friendly than buses.

Prediction after 100 years: I don’t think streetcars will be a thing. If the pure advantage of street cars is consistency, then we have other methods of ensuring that, such as automation. If we also consider the direct electric lines of power that dont require a battery to be an advantage, then it is quite feasible of us to open up a more general system that allows the empowerment of multiple different types of interfacing vehicles rather than the strict, route-focused streetcar. Unfortunately (fortunately?) this is a specter of a bygone past.
